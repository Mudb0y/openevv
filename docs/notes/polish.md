# Polish

`lang/plpl` exists and speaks. As it stands it is Italian under a Polish name -- made by copying `lang/itit`'s five text forms and its rules and renaming them -- and that is the chassis rather than a placeholder: from here every change has something audible on both sides of it. `NOTICE` says the licence consequence, which is that all of it is IBM's Italian data until it has been replaced.

It was built from the text forms and nothing else. No object is opened after the template's rules are written out, which is what those forms were for. It speaks the same samples as Italian, to the hash, on the same sentence; it holds up in one binary beside the other eight, each of the nine still saying exactly what it says alone; and English's 81 cases and samples are untouched.

**Polish has no oracle and it now has cases.** There is no IBM Polish, so nothing can be held against an original and nothing ever will be; for a while that meant Polish's behaviour was recorded in no place at all, and a change to it -- or a change elsewhere that broke it -- was invisible. `test/cases/plain-plpl.txt`, `utf8-plpl.txt` and `anno-plpl.txt` are 81 cases of its own now, written for Polish orthography rather than translated: the digraphs cz sz rz dz dź dż ch, the nasals ą and ę, ó against u, rz against ż, the retroflex series, and the diacritics both present and stripped the way people actually type. `test/matrix.sh` records what the engine says to each, so from here a change to Polish can at least be told from an accident. What it cannot be told from is a mistake -- that is still an ear, and `tools/measure/say.sh` is how to use one.

Italian is the template on phonetics rather than convenience. Its stress is predominantly penultimate as Polish's is almost always penultimate, its five vowels have no reduction, and its consonants carry the affricates ts and dz, tʃ and dʒ, the palatal nasal that is exactly Polish ń, and a trilled r. Spanish has half of that. What no module has is the vowel y; the nasal vowels can be read out of French and /x/ out of Spanish, since all eight are text now. The alveolo-palatals ś ź ć dź turned out not to be missing at all -- Italian's own S and Z are marked palatal in IBM's phoneme table, which is what that series is -- and what had to be found was somewhere to put the hard series instead.

Polish is family seventeen, `0x110000`. The family is not free: three tables are indexed by it and hold eighteen, IBM used six, and four more -- 6, 10, 11 and 16 -- are families its own `rz_isRomExist` says have a romanizer, so an instance of one is refused outright when the romanizer is absent. Family sixteen was tried first and `eciNewEx` answered -21.

The first rule written for Polish rather than lifted for Italian is in `lang/plpl/rules/is_val.up`: a locus rule, written in the upper form against names now carried in `plpl.globals`, where ten of the machine's variables are labelled as the formant targets a consonant is spoken with.

`make EVVLANG=lang/plpl census` says how much of the module is still Italian: 1,749 rules of 1,750 character for character, the settings two lines apart, the variables named. What it prevents is Italian phonology coming out of something labelled Polish without anyone noticing.

Polish's own letters are in it. A language's alphabet is the value names of the input statement's first field, and beside it in the same statement is a record of five bytes for each of those names: the character's case, whether it is a letter, whether it is a vowel, whether it carries an accent, and the phoneme it says on its own. That last is letter-to-sound at its simplest and it is data rather than code, which `tools/module/alphabet.py` now reads and writes by name.

The sixteen Polish letters went in at byte values the alphabet did not claim -- nineteen were free and sixteen were wanted -- rather than by reusing a code, since the dictionaries are keyed by code and moving one moves every word that used it. Each starts from the nearest phoneme the module has: ł says w, which is what Polish ł is, ń says the palatal nasal Italian spells gn and Polish spells ń, ć says C, ś says S, ź and ż say Z, ą says a and ę says e until the nasal vowels are read out of French. ó was already there.

Measured rather than assumed: before, a Polish letter cost about thirteen thousand samples wherever it stood, because the engine had no name for the byte and read it as a symbol -- eight of them alone came to 103,356 samples. After, `kąt` is 10,648 samples where `kat` is 8,107, and the two traces share their first 14,336 rule entries. A Polish letter on its own is still silent, because a lone letter is spoken by its name and Polish's letters have no names yet.

And Polish text arrives now. A caller writes code points and the machine reads single bytes, and IBM's engine does almost nothing between them: the code set only mattered under the SSML filter and for the four families with a romanizer, so on the ordinary path the caller's bytes are the characters. That was enough for the nine languages IBM shipped, every letter of which is in the Windows Western byte set, and not enough for one whose letters are not. `lang/<tag>/<tag>.codepoints` says what each of a language's own characters arrives as, `make codepoints` writes it into the module, and `addTextRun` converts on the way in.

That is a deliberate divergence, the fourth in the tree, and the guard is what makes it safe rather than merely careful: the conversion runs only for a language that declares characters of its own, and the nine IBM shipped declare none. What says so is not the argument but the suite -- English's 81 cases, German's 80 and the samples hash, on sixty-four bits and on thirty-two, all untouched.

Measured on the same pangram: `Zażółć gęślą jaźń` is 16,819 samples where it was 120,714, and `kąt` written as UTF-8 comes out byte for byte identical to `kąt` written in the module's own bytes, which is what says the conversion is exact rather than close.

The first change to how Polish sounds is in as well, and what it proves is worth more than what it does. Polish speaks sz, ż, cz and dż as retroflexes, further back than the palato-alveolars Italian spells with sc and gi, and a retroflex is a low third formant. `pol_retroflex_Fv` in `lang/plpl/rules/is_val.up` says that -- f3 down from Italian's 2400 to 2200, f2 from 1800 to 1700 -- and the two calls inside `ital_ph_S` and `ital_ph_Z` in Polish's own copy of the rules point at it. `sciarpa` spoken by Italian and by Polish is the same word at the same length, 10,197 samples each, with 17,448 of its 20,438 bytes identical: exactly one sound moved. That is the whole path from an authored rule to the samples, proved.

Two letters that make one sound are in, which is what a Polish word needed. Polish spells its retroflexes as digraphs -- sz, cz, rz, dz and dz with a dot -- and `lang/plpl/rules/it_phone.up` now has a rule for each of the five letters those start with, written in the upper form, with the chain in `it_phone.dr` that hands a letter to its rule pointed at them one call at a time.

The idiom is the machine's rather than ours. A letter rule is handed the two ends of the run it is being asked about and fills the range between them with phonemes, so a rule that says two letters make one sound moves the right-hand end past the second letter first -- `savescptr`, which puts where the scan has got to into the cell -- and answers that position back to its caller through the second pointer, so the next letter read is the one after the digraph. Italian's own rules do exactly that for ss and sci, which is where it was read from.

What it changes, word by word. `szafa` was s z a f a and is now the retroflex, a, f, a. `czas` was k dz a s -- Italian reads c before a consonant as k -- and is now the retroflex affricate, a, s. `rzeka` was r ts e k a and is now the voiced retroflex, e, k, a. `dzwon` was d dz v o n and is now dz, v, o, n. `dzem` with a dot was d, nothing, e, m and is now the voiced retroflex affricate, e, m. Polish c on its own is the affricate Italian spells z, so `cena` was tsh e n a and is ts e n a; Polish z on its own is a z, so `zima` was dz i m a and is z i m a. ch stays a k, which is what Italian does with it and as near as this inventory comes to Polish /x/, so `chleb` is k l e b.

And `las zima` keeps its s and its z apart. The scan that reads the next letter stops at the fence, so a word ending in s before one beginning with z is two words and not a digraph. That is `setscan_nof_r` rather than `setscan_r`, and it is the difference between one right answer and a rule that would have run words together for ever.

The eight letters Italian has not got speak now as well, and getting there took three things.

The first is that they arrive at all. They did not: IBM's romanizer runs every byte of the text through one 256-byte table when no corpus is loaded, and that table turns most of 0x80 to 0x9f into a space, those being undefined in the Western set it was made for -- which is exactly the range a new language's letters are free to take, since everything else is claimed. So a Polish word arrived as several one-letter ones and every Polish letter was a space: `dzem` with a dot came out as the letter d spoken by its Italian name and then em. `rz_convertText` now leaves a byte alone if the language in force claims it in its own `codepoints`, and that is the fifth deliberate divergence in the tree. The nine IBM shipped claim none, so for them the table decides as it always did.

The second is a link in the chain. A letter reaches its rule through a chain of alternatives in `generate_ital_diaphons`, one per letter: the chain sets the scan at the letter, asks whether it is this letter, and calls that letter's rule. A letter Italian never had has no link, and adding one means renumbering the tags the whole 1,057-line rule backtracks through and extending the table it dispatches on. So the link for q, which Polish does not use, is pointed at two rules of ours instead: one says whether the letter is any of the eight, the other speaks it. Eight questions in a row about the same letter cost nothing, because a one-character test that fails does not move the scan.

The third is that a rule standing where a wrapper stands has to be shaped like one. The first version was an ordinary rule -- landing place, `ventproc`, `succeed` -- and it committed the choice points the chain had planted around the call. Nothing was visibly wrong until a word with no vowel in it crashed several rules later in the durations. `bare` in the upper form is the answer: entered, and it answers, which is what all 1,037 of the language's own wrappers are.

What the letters say: l with a stroke is w, which is what Polish l with a stroke is; n with an acute is the palatal nasal Italian spells gn; s with an acute is the retroflex and c with an acute the retroflex affricate; z with an acute and z with a dot are both the voiced retroflex. The two ogoneks are nasal vowels, which this inventory has not got, and are spoken as the vowel and then an n -- which is what Polish itself does before a stop. Measured: `kat` with an ogonek is 8,712 samples, exactly what `kont` is, where `kat` is 8,107. `zaba` with a dot over the z is the same length as `zaba` without and a different sound, which is one letter moved and nothing else. `Zazolc geslq jazn` written properly says every one of its letters and is 20,240 samples where it was 16,819 with six of them silent.

Six of the eight go through wrappers the module already has, so a trace says which phoneme was laid down. The two ogoneks lay down two phonemes and no wrapper carries a pair, so they name their bytes in `lang/plpl/rules/constants` and call the primitive.

All three shapes say it the same. The thirty-two bit build and the Windows one agree with the sixty-four bit build byte for byte on `szafa`, `zaba` with a dot, `kat` with an ogonek and the pangram -- but only when the text is handed over in a file. On the command line the Windows build disagreed on every case with a Polish letter in it, and `zaba` with a dot came out as exactly the hash of `zaba` without: Wine converts a command line to the Windows code page on the way in and that page has no Polish. It is the harness and not the engine, and it is written down here because it reads exactly like a bug.

One fault is left and it is understood rather than guessed at. A lone Polish consonant -- a bare z with a dot, or s with an acute -- crashes. A word with no vowel in it is not spoken by the letter rules at all: it is spelled out letter by letter, which is why `psst` comes out as pi ti and `sz` on its own as zeta. A letter of Polish's own has no name to be spelled with, so the word ends with nothing to say and the durations are asked to shape an utterance that is not there. Every word with a vowel in it is fine, and so is any word whose first letter is one of Italian's. Two null guards in the machine were tried and taken back out: each moved the crash one dereference further on, which is what says the state is wrong rather than the pointer. The fix is the letter names, in the dictionary, and that is the next piece of work.

While the letters were being put in, `tools/module/alphabet.py` was found to have added every one of their records one slot too high. The alphabet is 207 names with 208 records, IBM's own trailing spare being the extra, and `add` was appending after that spare rather than before it -- so the sixteen Polish letters each read the record of the letter before, and the first of them read the spare, which says undefined for everything. Nothing had noticed because nothing yet read those records for a sound. The tool inserts before the spare now, and `lang/plpl/plpl.statements` has been repaired with the same bytes in the right order: l with a stroke says the glide again, n with an acute the palatal nasal, and the ogoneks their vowels.

There is no oracle for any of it -- IBM never made a Polish module -- so the ear is the judge, and a hash beside each verdict is what will keep an approved sound from moving afterwards. `tools/module/phonemes.py` is the map: every phoneme as the statements name it, the settings declare it and a rule speaks it, with the place each one is spoken at. Italian declares 35, gives 21 a rule, and `ital_pal_Fv` serves four of them -- which is the sort of thing to know before moving any of it. The tool that would let the ear be spared the structural part is `build/probe ... p`, which asks for phonemes instead of sound; it is one flag away from working and `docs/building.md` says which. Until it works, a trace read for the insert wrappers says what a word came out as, which is how every phoneme above was measured.

Then the two sibilant series were separated, which is the change with the most Polish in it so far.

Polish has two where Italian has one: the hard sz ż cz dż, said with the tongue curled back, and the soft ś ź ć dź, said with it flat against the palate. They are different letters and different words -- kasza is not Kasia -- and both were coming out of the same two engine sounds, so cześć had the same noise twice.

IBM's table has the distinction already. Every phoneme carries a record of eight bytes -- class, voicing, sonority, manner, place, and the three a vowel wants -- and place runs lab, alv, pal, vel, ret. Italian never uses ret, and its S and Z are pal, which is what the soft series is. So the soft series is Italian's own two as they stand and the hard series needed two phonemes of its own.

There was nowhere to put a new one, and that is the finding worth keeping. A phoneme code is tested by name in some five hundred places across a module: `ital_labial_Fv` alone asks about its neighbours 68 times, each vowel rule asks which consonant follows it upwards of fifty, and the durations, the transition durations and the syllabifier all ask. A code that has never existed is invisible to every one of them -- its neighbours coarticulated as if it were not there, its durations from nowhere, which is the same class of hole that crashes a lone Polish letter. A code that already exists has an arm in every chain.

So the hard series took over the two of Italian's that no Polish word can reach: L, its palatal l from gli, and R, its strong trill. Measured rather than assumed -- glina, kolano, lala, rok, brat, burza, rzeka, bank and ranking between them produce neither, ever, while the velar nasal that bank does produce is one Polish wants. Their records say fricative and retroflex now, written by name through `tools/module/phonemes.py set`, which reads the field list and the record's length out of the statement rather than assuming either.

No new value rule was needed. The value chain's two arms now call Italian's own fricative rules and the duration chain's call its fricative durations -- four call names -- so the hard series inherits the geminate handling, the amplitudes and the noise modulation a fricative already has. One decision is left for a rule and `pol_sibilant_Fv` is it: `ital_ph_S` and `ital_ph_Z` ask for a place, and it answers the retroflex one when the phone about to be spoken is either hard code and the palatal one otherwise. How a value rule finds its own phone is worth writing down: it loads the left register from the variable at state+832 and scans rightwards over the phone statement, which is what the value chain itself does to decide which rule to call.

Then the letters, and then the spellings, which is where the audible part is: ś as a letter is rare next to si. Polish softens s, z, c, dz and n before an i, and the i is a softener when a vowel follows it and a vowel when one does not -- Kasia is two syllables and dziś is one. `ZZtestFldeq1_4_1` asks whether the next letter is a vowel, and since each test leaves the scan past what it matched, `savescptr` after the z gives a dz span and after the i a dzi span. dzi has to be asked about before dz or dz swallows it.

Two more letters went the same way. Polish g is always a g, where Italian reads gn as the palatal nasal and gi as an affricate, so gniazdo had been coming out with no g in it. And n softens before an i like the rest, giving konie, niebo and nikt their ń. What a rule of ours loses by standing where Italian's stood was worth checking: Italian's n assimilates to the velar nasal before a k or a g and Polish wants that too, and it survives, because the assimilation is in the phonology rather than in the letter.

What comes out now: Kasia takes the soft locus and no hard one, kasza the reverse, cisza one of each, cześć one hard and two soft, szczęście two of each. zima is the soft ź and an i, which is what Polish says and not what it did.

And the numbers are the ear's. Three settings for the hard locus were built and listened to on the same five words -- f2 and f3 at 1700 and 2200, 1600 and 1900, 1800 and 2500 -- and the last was the one that sounded like Polish, which says the first two were too far back for this synthesiser rather than too far back for the language: Italian's own palatal locus is 1800 and 2400 and what won is a hair above it. The build in the tree says those five words byte for byte identically to the clip that was chosen, which is what says the number heard is the number that went in. The soft locus went the same way -- 2000 and 2700, 2100 and 3000, 2200 and 3200, with the hard pair held still so that only one sound moved -- and the highest won there too, which leaves the two series seven hundred hertz apart in the third formant. That is what keeps kasza and Kasia from collapsing back together, and it is a wider gap than the guessed numbers had.

Then the vowels, where the same arithmetic came out again. Italian has seven and Polish six, and the two Italian has spare are exactly the two Polish does not want: the close-mid e and the close-mid o. Polish e is ɛ wherever it stands and Polish o is ɔ, where Italian chooses between each pair by stress and syllable shape, so those two letter rules are one insert each and no tests. o with an acute is u -- mój is muj and sól is sul, where both had an ɔ in them. And j is the glide, where Italian's is an affricate, so mój had been ending in a dż.

Which leaves the close-mid e with nothing producing it, and that is where y went. Polish y is ɨ, a sound no module has, and speaking it as an i costs minimal pairs: być is not bić and syn is not sen. `stanital_ph_e` carries its formants now, which is five numbers in one rule -- the vowel rules are 524 lines of context tests and ten stores, and only five of those stores are the targets. Those five went to the ear the way the sibilants' did: f1, f2 and f3 at 400, 1600 and 2500, at 400, 1400 and 2400, and at 350, 1800 and 2600, on być, bić, syn, sen, ryba, dobry syn and tylko ty. The last won, which puts ɨ nearer i than the textbook would and still keeps być and bić apart -- and the build in the tree says those seven words byte for byte identically to the clip that was chosen.

Taking a vowel over needed one thing the sibilants did not. `make_ital_phon_adjustments` has three arms that put one phoneme in place of another: the first turns an n before a k into the velar nasal, which is why bank is bank and which Polish wants, and the other two close a mid vowel. Those two now put back the vowel they were given, which is both what says Polish has no such alternation and what actually frees the close-mid e -- without it, teraz came out with ɨ in the middle where the letter rule had put an ɛ. Each is IBM's own wrapper with one symbol changed, written as raw lower notation inside a `bare` rule so that nothing else about it can differ, and put back rather than cut out: the same insert of the same length happens at the same point, so nothing downstream can tell that anything was skipped.

So teraz is t ɛ r a z, kolano is k ɔ l a n ɔ, okno is ɔ k n ɔ, dobry is d ɔ b r ɨ, mówię is m u v j ɛ n, and bank still has its velar nasal. The census reads 98% Italian: 1,742 rules of 1,767 character for character, 7 changed and 18 ours alone.

And the letters have their own names, which is what the lone-consonant crash was. A lone letter is spoken by spelling its name out as characters and reading that back as a word, and `ital_char_name` is the dispatch that does it: 205 arms behind a bound test, so Polish's letters at codes 207 to 222 fell off the end of it and the word was left with nothing to say.

Six of them needed a name -- ć, ł, ń, ś, ź and ż, since a lone vowel says the phoneme in its own alphabet record and ą and ę were already speaking -- and 118 of that rule's arms are three lines: the statement type and the count packed into one register, the characters in another, and a jump to the block that inserts them. So six arms of the same three lines went in, the bound went from 204 to 210, and the switch lists 211. cie, eł, eń, eś, ziet and żet, every one of them spelled with letters that already speak, and each spelled name reads back through the letter rules -- the ż inside żet is a letter in a word rather than a lone one, so nothing recurses.

The entries themselves are the dictionary's: six lines in `lang/plpl/plpl.dict` with the action numbers those arms answer to, laid down by `tools/module/dict.py build` and then dumped back into `plpl.sets` so the text carries them and `make tables-write` cannot lose them.

Doing it that way rather than letting the tool mint the arms was not a preference. `tools/module/dict.py` can mint one -- it did, and read its own work back -- but the arm it writes names its record by an address in the program, and since the engine stopped needing to be loaded low, an address the arena does not know is a crash. So the tool's write path and the arena scheme have not met, and until they do an arm belongs in the rules text, where `rules/constants` puts the bytes somewhere the arena is told about. That is one of the two things left in the dictionary tooling; the other is that nothing can yet remove an entry, which is what emptying Italian's abbreviations will want.

Then the whole Latin alphabet got Polish names, and Italian's words went.

The two go together and the first forced the second. Twenty-six arms of the same three lines, so a is a rather than ah -- which sounds right today only because h is silent and will stop sounding right the moment /x/ exists -- be, ce, de, ef, gie, ha, jot, ka, el, em, en, pe, ku, er, es, te, faw, wu, iks, igrek and zet. Then c came out as tɕɨ, because the name ce is an Italian function word saying tʃe, and the name was being read as a word rather than as letters. So the letter names cannot be right while Italian's words are in the module.

`lang/plpl/plpl.dict` is the source the tables are laid down from, so removing an entry needs no feature at all: a line dropped from the text is an entry the build does not lay down. Eleven of the thirteen dictionaries are empty now -- 630 entries of Italian function words, roots, abbreviations, tokens, loanwords, clitics and homograph rules -- and what that fixed is more than the names: dom is d ɔ m and not domenica, co is ts ɔ, ok is ɔ k.

One of the twelve cannot be empty and that is worth knowing before emptying anything else. With `prod_prefixes` empty, procent and protokol crash in the durations while produkt and prosty do not, which is the walk-from-nowhere the lone letters used to hit. Keeping its 61 entries costs nothing measurable -- co and coraz still say ts ɔ -- and they are the Latin and Greek prefixes Polish borrowed as well: auto, anti, inter, mono, post, pre, pro, super, tele, trans. So they stay, and the fault they hide is written down here rather than guarded against. Since 29 September they are never asked at all: the paragraph at the end says why.

What that leaves audibly wrong in this area: numbers. 123 is centoventitré and 10 is dieci, because the number rules spell a digit into Italian words in the rules rather than in the dictionary, and emptying ital_numbers did not touch them.

And Polish counts. The digits, the teens, the tens, the hundreds, the thousands and the millions, which is everything up to a billion: 1999 is tysiąc dziewięćset dziewięćdziesiąt dziewięć, 2026 is dwa tysiące dwadzieścia sześć, 1234567 is milion dwaset trzydzieści cztery tysiące pięćset sześćdziesiąt siedem.

`it_numbr.dr` is 26 rules over nine thousand lines and covers dates, times, money, Roman numerals, ordinals and fractions as well as counting, but the words are strings a rule hands to a wrapper whose name carries their length, and a word goes in as characters which the letter rules then read -- the same path a letter's name takes. So a Polish word is a constant of ours plus, at the call site, the symbol and the length, and which arm fires for which digit never changes. `pol_insert_<n>` and `pol_insert_l_<n>` in `lang/plpl/rules/it_numbr.up` are the reusable half of that: IBM compiled one insert wrapper per length its own words needed, and these are the lengths Polish's need.

Two things had to be solved rather than typed. Italian writes ventitré as one word and drops the tens' final vowel before uno, where Polish writes two words; rather than touch the rules that join, every tens word carries a trailing space, so the digit after it lands in a word of its own. And Italian's tens reach their insert through a block per length, three words to a block, where Polish's eight tens are eight different lengths, so five of them got a four-line block of their own and the dispatch was not touched at all.

Three faults on the way, all the same lesson: a word's length is stated in more places than the call site. A direct insert has the length and the statement type adjacent on the stack, and changing the wrong one put fifty into statement thirteen. A second arm jumps into the block whose length had been raised, still carrying Italian's shorter word, so 101 read past the end of it and crashed while 102 did not. And an arm takes its word from a register set two hundred lines earlier, so 1001 said tysiąc jede. Each was found by bisecting what had changed rather than by reading it.

What is still Italian in the numbers: two, three and four hundred, which Polish makes irregularly -- dwieście, trzysta, czterysta -- where five hundred through nine hundred are regular and come out right, so 200 says dwaset; the plurals, since tysiąc is tysiące for two to four and tysięcy above, and this always says tysiące; the leading digit of two billion; and ordinals, dates, times, money and Roman numerals, which are each their own rule, so 1. is still primo.

The eight capitals of Polish's own letters were silent, which no test here had caught because every Polish word in them started with a letter Italian has. Żaba said aba, Śnieg said śnieg without the ś, and ŁÓDŹ -- nothing but silent letters -- crashed, the way a word with nothing to say always does. The letter chain dispatches on a character code and never saw them, because `convert_to_lowercase` has no arm for them: it is 62 arms, one per letter it knows, and each arm plants the tag of the next, so adding eight means renumbering the chain rather than appending to a table.

They arrive as their own lowercase instead: eight lines in `lang/plpl/plpl.codepoints`. That is right for every sound and wrong only for anything that would want to know a letter was capital, which nothing in the module does yet. The proper fix is the eight arms, and the reason it was not done that way is written above the lines.

And Polish has its nasal vowels, which no module had.

What made them possible is that the engine already nasalises: `nasalize_vow` in us_filtr.dr nasalises a vowel standing next to a nasal consonant, and it does it by writing **one** global -- a frequency taken from another and raised by 150. So a nasal vowel is a vowel whose own rule writes that global without waiting for a consonant to follow. Two wrong guesses came first and both are worth recording. The parameter the nasal consonants set and the vowels never touch does nothing at all when a vowel sets it -- the audio came back byte for byte identical, and a deliberate sabotage of the same block proved the block runs. And marking a vowel's record as manner "nas" does change the sound, but it makes every rule that asks treat it as a consonant, which sounded nasal and piercing at once.

ę cost no new phoneme code. Every Polish o is the open ɔ, so the close-mid o had nothing producing it, exactly as the close-mid e had nothing producing it before y took it: `stanital_ph_o` now carries ɛ's formants and the nasalisation, and the value and duration chains already had arms for it.

ą had nothing left to take, so it is the first genuinely new phoneme in the module, code 35, added by `tools/module/phonemes.py add`. Rather than copy five hundred lines of the open o's rule, its arm in `stanital_vals` calls that rule, and two small rules of ours decide the rest: one answers whether the phone about to be spoken is ą, and the other writes the nasalisation if it is. So the plain ɔ and the nasal ɔ share every line of their machinery and differ in one global. kąt and kot are the same phonemes in the same order and different sounds, which is what says it fires.

What that cost is worth knowing before the next new code: a code that has never existed is invisible to every rule that asks what its neighbours are, and what saves it here is that the syllabifier and the stress rules read the phone's *record* rather than its code, and the record says vowel.

And Polish devoices at the end of a word, which is the rule that was making every second word sound foreign: raz is ras, chleb is chlep, róg is ruk, rów is ruf, kod is kot, mąż is mąsz, lekarz is lekasz, wódz is wóts and Łódź is Łuć. Nine consonants do it -- b, d, g, w, z, ż, ź, rz, dz and dź -- and razem, ludzi, woda, dobry and ryba keep their voice, since the rule is about the end of a word and not about the letter.

It also devoices in front of a voiceless consonant, which is the commoner of the two: wtorek is ftorek, wszystko is fszystko, wódka is wutka, kredka is kretka, babka is bapka. That came free -- the same helper answers both questions, so every arm that already called it got the assimilation without another line. What it does not do is carry the devoicing leftwards through a whole cluster: gwóźdź comes out with a voiced ź where Polish devoices that too.

`pol_devoices_here` is the whole of it and it took three wrong tests to write. The scan can always be set, so a scan that fails is not the signal. `advance_tok` succeeds there too. And `testFldeq` answers "no" both when what follows is a letter and when nothing follows at all, so one test of it cannot tell the end of a word from the middle: raz before a space devoiced and raz at the end of everything did not. What works is both halves together -- the scan failing, or what follows being none of the four things a letter's record can say it is. The nine voiceless letters are tested in the same rule, before those four.

Two process notes, because both cost a build each. `make authored` was being run with its output silenced, so when the compiler refused the file -- a place named in one rule and defined in another -- three builds in a row were the same binary and the change looked like it had done nothing. And an edit that lands in the wrong arm of the right rule is invisible until the word that distinguishes them: the word-end test went into the dzi arm rather than the dź one, where it would have turned ludzi into lutsi.

And ch and h say /x/, where ch was a k and h was silent: chleb, herbata, hałas, chcę, mucha, duch.

/x/ is the module's second new phoneme, code 36, and it needed no rule of its own. It is a voiceless fricative, which is what Italian's own S rule makes, differing only in where it is spoken -- so the arm added to `ital_con_vals` hands it to that rule, and `pol_sibilant_Fv` gained a third answer: the velar place, which is Italian's own, the one k and g are spoken at. So the hard sibilant, the soft one and /x/ share every line of their machinery and differ in which locus they ask for. It fell through the duration chain without a crash, and that was taken for needing nothing there. It was not: falling through meant it was given no length at all, so /x/ was in the phoneme report and nowhere in the sound until 28 September, and the paragraph at the end says how it was found and mended.

What is left after this: Polish's own words, of which the module now has none -- the one-letter prepositions w and z are letter names rather than /v/ and /z/, since a lone letter is spelled before any word dictionary is consulted -- the numbers, the nasal vowels, currently a vowel plus an n, which is what Polish does before a stop and not elsewhere, /x/ for ch and h, which no module has, stress, which is Italian's penultimate rule and near but not the same, and prosody, which is Italian's outright.

And prosody has numbers under it now rather than an opinion. Judging intonation by ear says only that something sounds Italian, which is a statement about two things at once, so `tools/measure/pitch.py` measures instead: it tracks the fundamental frequency of a wav and reduces the contour to what intonation is made of -- where the voice sits, how wide it ranges, how far it drifts down across a phrase, how it starts and how it ends -- with every height in semitones from that speaker's own median, so that a man and a woman with the same melody come out the same. The same tool reads a recording of a person and reads our own output, which is the only reason the two can be held against each other.

Two things it needed before it could be believed. A tracker that decides each frame on its own reads a woman an octave low for whole phrases at a time, because a voice periodic at one interval is just as periodic at twice it; so every frame offers all its candidates, the path is chosen over the utterance at once, and a second pass is offered only the candidates near the median the first pass found. What says that is right is that the melody stops depending on the speaker's sex: over 335 read Polish sentences the men and the women now agree to within a tenth of a semitone on the range, on how far above the middle the voice goes, on how it starts and on how tall its accents are. And Praat, given the same two-pass treatment, agrees with it on all twenty files it was checked against, including the one woman whose voice really does sit at 110 hertz where a fixed floor had put her at 208.

The reference is Google's FLEURS, read prose in a hundred and two languages, of which Polish, US English and Italian were taken -- 338, 394 and 391 recordings by many speakers of each. `NOTICE` says whose it is. None of it is in the tree: what is kept is the numbers it yielded.

What human Polish does, over 335 statements. It ranges four semitones between its tenth and ninetieth percentile. It starts a semitone and a half above its own median and holds that for the first tenth of the phrase. It drifts down about a fifth of a semitone a second from there, ending a semitone below the middle, having fallen about a semitone and a half over the last half second. Its accents are 4.1 a second and 2.3 semitones tall.

Which settles whether to borrow English's melody, and the answer is no. Human English ranges 7.5 semitones where Polish ranges 4.1, its accents are 3.2 semitones against 2.3, it declines twice as steeply and starts twice as high. Polish is the flattest of the three measured -- flatter than Italian, at 4.8. Sounding Italian is not Polish wanting a wider melody.

What ours did instead, and in all three of its languages rather than only Polish: nothing for eighty-five per cent of the phrase and then a cliff. Averaged over 140 sentences and stretched to a common length, the contour sat within a tenth of a semitone of its own median from the quarter mark to the last twentieth, then fell three and a half semitones in that last twentieth -- five semitones over the final three hundred milliseconds, where a Pole falls one and a half over five hundred. Our English and our Italian do the same, so this is the shared machinery and not Italian's tables. And immediately before the plunge there was a rise, a hump of two thirds of a semitone about half a second from the end, which is a late peak of exactly the Italian kind.

Nine numbers in `lang/plpl/rules/is_inton.dr` were what did it, and about seventeen of their units make a semitone, which is measured rather than assumed: the sweep that found them rebuilds in nine seconds with `RULES=bytecode` and speaks all 140 sentences again, so the mapping from a number in a rule to a semitone of audio is a table now. Raising the low targets of `ital_phrase_tone` and of the nuclear falling accent, and lowering that accent's high targets so the hump goes, brings the end of a Polish statement to a semitone and a half below the middle over the last half second: the ramp a Pole makes rather than a step. Questions are untouched, and that is measured too -- `Czy mówisz po polsku?` ends a semitone above its median before the change and after it. The bytecode build and the rules-as-C build give the same numbers to two decimal places.

What is still wrong is the other eighty-five per cent. There is no declination at all: a person descends steadily across a whole phrase and ours is level until the last half second, so a long sentence has no shape. The accents are too few, 2.9 a second against 4.1, and too shallow, 1.75 semitones against 2.3. And the rhythm differs in one thing that can be measured without anybody marking up a recording: the spacing of the syllable nuclei taken from the loudness envelope. Polish syllables are more even than Italian's or English's -- 51 on the pairwise variability index against 57 for both -- where ours scores 56, which is Italian's figure and not Polish's.

And Polish stress is Polish now, which was the largest single thing making the language sound Italian. Every word ending in a consonant was accented on its last syllable, and in Polish that is a great many words: szesnastym came out szesnaSTYM where it is szesNAStym.

What locates the stress is `assign_root_stress`; `assign_primary_stress` does nothing else at all but call it and insert the mark where it left the scan. Italian's locator is a tree of eighty alternatives with a hundred and sixteen more beside it in `antepenult_stress`, which makes seventy string comparisons and is nothing but a list of the words that break the rule, because Italian stress is lexical and has to be listed word by word. And that tree splits on how a word ends. Fourteen words were traced and the split is exact: woda, kolano, herbata, dziewczyna, mucha, ryba and dobry take one path through it and nazywam, szesnastym, dworzec, uniwersytet, lekarz, poniedziałek and kot the other. Italian words end in vowels almost without exception, so the second path is barely exercised there.

`pol_primary_stress` in `lang/plpl/rules/it_strss.up` replaces the pair. It counts the syllables by walking the word at statement type seven, starts again, and inserts the mark over the penultimate. Sixteen lines against Italian's thousand and ninety, because Polish has nothing to look up: the penultimate syllable, always.

**For a month it marked the last syllable instead, and a pitch measurement said it was right.** The walk starts outside the word, so its count is one more than the syllables there are, and the mark covers what lies after its left end: stopping two short of that count, which is what it did, marks the last syllable. The phoneme report said so on every word -- mama as `.0ma.1ma`, woda as `.0vc.1da` -- while Italian's report from the same printer puts casa's stress on its first syllable. It stops three short now, and the report puts every word's stress on the penultimate.

What hid it is how Italian ends a statement. Its nuclear accent is `ital_nucl_high_low_star_tone`, a high on the syllable before the mark and a low on the marked one, and Italian shows it on its own words: the report marks the penultimate and the pitch peaks on the antepenultimate. So a mark on the last syllable put a peak on the penultimate of a sentence's last word, and that was all the check measured, since nonsense words of two to five syllables said on their own are each the last word of a sentence. Stopping one short gave no mark at all, the insertion failing and the rule giving up, and a flat contour rising to its end read as a final stress. Each step moved the peak by one syllable, which is why the sweep looked like proof.

Every other word takes its accent on the mark itself, so every word but the last was audibly stressed on its last syllable. In `tatata tatata tatata` the pitch peaked on the third syllable where Polish has the second, fifth and eighth, and it peaks on those now. The start of a sentence says the same without anything marked up: a person starts a statement a semitone and a half above their median, and over twenty statements ours started a quarter of a semitone below it with the mark on the last syllable and a little over one above it with the mark where it belongs, because the first word's accent now comes early rather than on its final syllable.

**Read stress from the phoneme report, never from where a pitch peak is.** An accent is anchored to the mark and can put its peak on a neighbour. `DELTA_RULE_TRACE` on a probe says which accent rule ran.

With the mark right, Italian's nuclear accent put its peak a syllable early in the last word, so do Krakowa rose on kra. Polish statements end on `ital_nucl_high_star_low_tone` instead, which is Italian's nuclear high on the marked syllable with the fall left to the phrase tone, and the dispatcher `nucl_high_low_star_tone` in `lang/plpl/rules/u_integ.dr` is what calls it. That puts every accent of a statement on the penultimate, and it was chosen by ear over the corrected mark with Italian's accent kept. It is the gentler of the two, about a semitone on nonsense words against nearly two.

Two things learned on the way are worth more than the fix. A rule cannot answer through `get_parm_ptr`: it copies the caller's value into a local of the callee's and pushes a pointer to that local, so a `savescptr` into it writes nothing the caller will ever read. Four separate experiments appeared to be moving the stress and were all of them no-ops, and what caught it was holding them against a rule whose entire body was `match`. The general form of that check is worth keeping: when a change to a rule seems to work, compare it against a rule that does nothing, not only against the rule it replaced.

And prominence in this engine is pitch and not length. In szesnastym the syllable stym runs a quarter of a second where na runs a seventh of one, which reads as stress and is not: stym has four phonemes and na has two. With the stress mark removed altogether the lengths do not move at all and the pitch goes flat. A duration is not evidence about stress, and an amplitude envelope is no use either -- a formant synthesiser barely dips between syllables, so the syllable detector that works on a corpus of people found one syllable in nazywam.

Three things are known to be left, in the order they are worth trying. The syllabifier is still Italian's, so a rule that correctly asks for the penultimate syllable can still be handed the wrong count: `create_ital_syllables` knows nothing of Polish consonant clusters, and szesnastym, poniedziałek and dziewczyna are exactly the shapes where it would differ. Secondary stress was not touched at all -- `assign_secondary_stress` still calls Italian's locator, with Italian's lexical exceptions inside it. And the accents are too few against the corpus, with no declination across the phrase at all: 2.9 a second at 1.75 semitones over the 140 sentences measured when the terminal fall was set, where a Pole has 4.1 at 2.3, and 2.7 a second at 2.6 semitones over the twenty statements measured once the mark had moved, a different set and so not a change. A one-syllable preposition took a stress of its own as well, so do in do Krakowa was accented where Polish leans it on the word after; that is mended now, at the end.

The end of a Polish statement was also taken a semitone deeper than the corpus figure, by ear. Matching a person's terminal fall exactly left it sounding unfinished, which is a limit on the whole method worth stating: the numbers say where to aim and what has moved, and they do not say when it is right.

And w and z are prepositions now rather than letter names. Idę w dom z bratem had been idę wu dom zet bratem, in some form in most sentences Polish has, because a token with no vowel reaches `letter_sequence`, which tries it as an acronym and then as a lone letter and spells the lone letter by name. Italian has no word of one consonant, so it never needed to do otherwise.

Polish says each as part of the word after it, so that is what is done: `pol_tok_lookup` in `lang/plpl/rules/ut_norm.up` stands where `tok_lookup` stood in `normalize_text`, which is before anything has read the token, and when the token is w or z of either case followed by spaces and a word of two letters or more it lays the letter back down over itself and the spaces, which is how this machine takes a character out -- `merge_adjacent_spaces` does the same with a run of spaces. Then it hands over to `tok_lookup` as before. The pair is an ordinary word from there on, and everything else is rules that already existed: w domu is stressed where domu is, w polu and w ścianie are f polu and f ścianie and z tobą is s tobą, because a consonant takes its voice from the one after it, and w łóżku, z żoną and w Łodzi join the same way. Kraków domu is untouched, since the w there does not begin a token.

Where the word after is one letter, or nothing follows, they stay letter names: litera w i litera z means the letters, and so does z a i b. Before a number they are still spelled, w 2020 roku among them, because the digits become words only later and a w joined to 2020 would be a token of letters and digits, which is spelled out as well. The 22 of Polish's 98 cases that moved are exactly the ones with a w or z before a word, and what the interface answered on them moved only in how many samples they came to.

Polish has a word list now, and the first thing it did was find eight words the engine could not say. `test/cases/words-plpl.txt` is twenty thousand words out of LibreOffice's Polish dictionary, and recording it hung: four of six processes sat at a full core for half an hour. Each of the eight hung the phoneme report on its own, and every one of them had done so before the stress and the prepositions were changed as well.

Six were the Italian enclitics. Italian reads a word ending in mi, ti, li, la or le after one of its verbs as the verb and an attached pronoun, and `enclitics` in `it_morph.dr` strips the ending and says it out of a dictionary of its own -- one of those emptied of Italian. So a Polish word that happens to spell an Italian verb and a pronoun lost most of itself: tonami and stanami, the plurals of ton and stan, came out as a third of a second of sound, and the phoneme report never came back from them at all, because `stb_sendPhonemesToUser` goes round for ever when the engine fails, which is IBM's and noted where it is. `pol_no_enclitics` in `lang/plpl/rules/it_morph.up` stands where `enclitics` was called and answers that no word ends in one.

The other two had no vowel and an ł in them. ł was recorded as a glide, the class Italian's y is in, and Italian counts a glide as enough to make a word pronounceable -- py is one syllable there. So a Polish token of consonants and ł was taken for a word rather than spelled, and the word it made had nothing to build a syllable on, since ł says w. It is a consonant in Polish spelling and it is recorded as one now, it and its capital, which `tools/module/alphabet.py set` writes by name; pł is spelled pe eł and ładny, głowa and Łódź say exactly what they said.

Neither change moved one of the 98 cases or one of the other 20,252 words, which was measured by speaking the whole list through the build before and after them.

And Polish's letter-to-sound is a file a person edits now: `lang/plpl/letters`, in the notation Spanish and Italian were written in, compiled by `tools/rules/letters.py` into `rules/it_phone.up` the way theirs are. It says exactly what the seventeen rules written by hand in the upper form said, which were in that file until it was generated, and it replaces them.

It took three lines of notation the other languages had not needed. `voiceless means p t k f s c h ś ć and the end of the word` names a class that an arm asks for as it would a kind, so devoicing is one arm -- `b before voiceless says p` -- where it had been a helper every letter called; the end of the word counts as one of the class, which is why raz is ras and babka is bapka by the same arm, and it compiles to exactly the helper's test, the scan that cannot be set or the character that is no kind of letter. `ą ć ę ł ń ś ź ż come through q` says those eight have no link of their own in the dispatcher, so their blocks become arms of one rule standing where q's stood, with a test beside it for the dispatcher to ask, which is what `pol_test_own_letters` and `apply_pol_letter_rules` were by hand. And `phone sz is L` and eleven lines like it name the phones Polish took over by Polish spelling, since `sz says L` reads as something it is not. The vowels keep the engine's names because e, o, y and c are phones already.

f, p, t, v, k, x, a, l, m, i and u were Italian's when the file was written, copied from `lang/itit/letters` so that every letter is in one file, and two of Italian's i arms turned out to be looser than IBM's rule in a way no Italian word can show. The arm for -ismo and -ista made the i a vowel before any st or sm, where IBM wants a vowel after them as well: ateista, ateismo, ateistą and ateistó have the vowel and ateistka and oleisty the glide, y being a glide in this alphabet. And the arm for celiachia made it a vowel before any ach, where IBM wants ache or achi: gapiach and głasnostiach glide. Eleven words of the list moved until the two were tightened, and asking IBM's compiled rule about -ist-, -ism- and -iach- before every letter and every accented vowel is what said which. `lang/itit/letters` has the same two arms and Italian cannot hear the difference, which is why it has not been changed.

What says the file is the rules it replaced: the 98 cases and all 20,260 words of the list are as they were in both builds, and the sound gate, every word spoken through the build before and the build after with the samples compared, is what holds it where the phoneme report cannot see a phone's marks.

Then the first change the letters file made rather than kept: w and rz lose their voice after a voiceless consonant as well as before one. przy, trzy, krzesło and przez had a voiced ż where Polish has sz, and kwiat, twój, świat, chwila and czwartek a v where it has an f. It is the one direction of assimilation that works forwards, and only those two letters do it. Two arms, `w after voiceless says f` and `rz after voiceless says sz`, with sz and cz added to the class, since czwartek and szwagier would otherwise be read as a z before the w. A class member of two letters is read nearest letter first when it is asked for behind the letter. 1,262 words of the list moved and 1,260 of them have a w or rz straight after a voiceless letter; the other two are a number and a symbol whose spoken form has trzy in it. The 21 words with that shape that did not move already said sz or f, the rz or w there ending the word or standing before another voiceless consonant. 29 of the 98 cases moved, every one for przy, przez, przed, trzy, chwile or świat.

By ear it is right with one reservation, and the reservation is the engine's rather than the rule's: trzy sounds closer to czy than it should. This engine has no affricate timing at all. Italian's C is split into a t and its fricative before any duration is decided, and ćma comes out sample for sample the same as the same word spelled with a t and an ś. Polish's cz was always a t and sz laid down by the letter rule, for want of a code, so trzy and czy are now the same two phones with the same timing, where a Polish czy is one sound with a short closure and a trzy is a t released before the sz. `diaph_ghost`, which the split sets on an affricate's two halves, is not the answer: it is read by the phoneme report to write the pair as one symbol and by nothing that times them, and marking cz with it changed no sample and made the report spell czy with ć's letter.

And /x/ is heard now, which it had not been since it was added. Czechów came out as czeów and drzwiach as drzwia, and a word with ch or h in it was shorter than the same word without it -- chleb against leb, duch against du -- because `ital_cons_durs`, which gives every consonant its length, has an arm for each of Italian's phones and none for code 36. The phone fell off the end of that chain with no duration, and a phone with no duration is not spoken; the phoneme report still named it, since the report is made before anything is timed, which is why nothing had said so. It has the arm the value chain already had, spliced in behind p's the same way: the phone is asked whether it is /x/, and if so it is timed by Italian's S rule, the voiceless fricative it is spoken as. Each /x/ is about 120 ms of noise now, as loud as an sz. 15 of the 98 cases moved, every one for a ch or an h it speaks -- złotych, pchnąć, żółtych, polskich, chropowatość, chwile, and the h of an annotation read as text -- and no word of the list, since the phoneme report does not say how long anything is.

The splice first planted the next arm's tag before asking, as the value chain's does, and the cases were the same with and without it. It does not now, since a tag planted before a question that fails is left behind for a later backtrack to find.

Then it sounded like sz rather than h, which was to be expected once it could be heard at all. /x/ had been handed to Italian's S rule, the voiceless fricative ś is spoken with, and changing where it is made changes its formant targets and nothing about its noise. The noise is the synthesiser's parallel branch, whose seven levels the rules keep in shorts 380 and 383 to 389 -- the frication at 2754, then A2, A3, A4, A5, A6 and the bypass at 2770 to 2790, in the order `do_fric_amps` is handed them -- and S sets A3 to A5 high, which is a sibilant. A velar fricative wants its noise low, about the second formant, and nothing above.

Setting those after calling Italian's rule changed no sample, and the trace said why: S hands its noise to the synthesiser in `modulate_noise` before it returns. So `pol_ph_x` in `lang/plpl/rules/is_val.up` is that rule's own steps with /x/'s numbers, the velar locus and then the noise and then `modulate_noise`, leaving out only the question S asks about a doubled consonant after it, which ch never is. The low bands pass far less noise than the high ones, so the first settings were nearly silent: A2 at 60 gave a tenth of an sz's loudness. Three louder ones went to the ear and the middle one won -- frication 60, A2 75, A3 68, A4 55, nothing higher -- about half as loud as sz with its zero-crossings at 0.35 where sz's are at 0.60. By ear it is better and not yet right. The same 15 cases moved as when /x/ was given its length, and no word of the list.

It was distinctly an h and harsh with it -- like an English sh said with a heavy lisp, by ear -- and a spectrum over the noise alone said why: the peak was at 1 to 2 kHz where it should be, and 2 to 3 kHz was only 1.7 dB below it, a bump in the middle of the range where sh has its peak. A Polish ch is dark and diffuse. It is now A2 76, A3 62, A4 nothing and the bypass 50, which leaves everything above 2 kHz level at 12 to 14 dB down with nothing sticking out; `modulate_noise` was not the harshness, since all it does is swell the frication five above its level in the middle and five below at either end. Chosen by ear from three and called pretty good.

And trzy is not czy now, nor drzewo dżem. The two were the same phones with the same timing, and the release is what a cluster has that an affricate has not -- the stop let go before the fricative starts -- so the t of trz and the d of drz are given ten milliseconds more of it than Italian's rule gives them. Chosen by ear from nothing, ten, twenty and thirty-five more.

What tells the duration rules which stops those are is a mark the letter rules set: `t before rz says t marked tenseness tense` in `lang/plpl/letters`, and the same for d. Tenseness is a vowel's field; every phoneme's record says `~tense` and no rule of the module reads it, so `tense` on a consonant is a flag nothing else will see, and with the release left as it was every case and word came out sample for sample as before. `pol_ph_t_dur` and `pol_ph_d_dur` in `lang/plpl/rules/is_dur.up` stand where `ital_cons_durs` called Italian's t and d rules, run them, and add to the release in the short at 2988, whose value is at 2990, when the stop is marked.

Two routes failed first and both are worth not trying again. Marking the stop `asp` does nothing, because Italian's `ital_trans_features` sets every phone's transition afterwards, and when a rule was put in to keep the mark it turned out Italian sets `asp` itself on a t before a pause -- kot changed. And reading the letters from the point before the phone, which is where the phone's own letters begin since a letter rule lays its phones over exactly its letters' range, works at the start of a word and nowhere else: further in, once the syllables are made, the scan can no longer be set on the letters from there. The rule adds to Italian's release rather than setting it, having first set it to ten, which is Italian's value for these stops almost everywhere and was taken for everywhere: the t of a trzy inside a number is given twenty, and a case moved that should not have.

13 of the 98 cases moved, every one for a 3 said as trzy, trzydzieści or trzecia, and no word of the list, the phoneme report saying nothing of time.

And i is Polish's now, four arms where Italian's was forty: the vowel after a vowel, the glide between a consonant and a vowel, the vowel anywhere else, and the accented i as before. Polish writes its glide j, so an i after a vowel is a vowel -- naiwny is na-iw-ny, boisko bo-is-ko, Ukraina u-kra-i-na -- where Italian glided it; and an i between a consonant and a vowel is the glide, which Italian's word lists and stressed endings had made a vowel in anomalia, historii and every -logia, -metria and -grafia, with the stress a syllable late in all of them. Heard and called much better. 282 words of the list moved, 204 an i after a vowel and 78 one before, and no case. The 204 include loanwords Polish says with a glide whatever the spelling -- Adelaide, airways, alzheimer -- which are a dictionary's to say rather than a letter's.

Words beginning bi and a vowel still have the vowel, and not because of i. Italian's prefix list, kept because emptying it crashed procent, has bi in it, so biały, bieg and biuro are read as a prefix and a root and the glide is not asked for across the seam. Taking bi out of the list does not mend them: every such word then comes out with no sound at all, under Italian's i as much as Polish's, because the rule that strips prefixes tests for them by itself and falls over when the list no longer has the one it found. The same list costs surowy its stress.

And a syllable does not begin in the middle of a cz any more. And a word's last consonant takes its voice from the next word now. Polish devoices at the end of a word only where nothing voiced follows: before a word beginning with b, d, g, z, ź or ż a voiced final keeps its voice and a voiceless one gains it -- pod domem keeps its d, jak dobrze is jag, kot biega is kod, nasz dom has a ż and noc była a dz. A vowel, a sonorant, a pause or a punctuation mark after the word leaves it voiceless as before, and so do w and rz, which are voiced without passing the voice on: brat wie keeps its t, pod wodą its t. A cluster takes the voice whole, so the arms for st, ść, szcz and kt swallow the rest of it: jest dobrze is jezd, sześć złotych szeźdź.

It is two lines of notation. `unless voiced follows` ending the voiceless class says the end of a word is not voiceless when the next word begins with one of `voiced means b d g z ź ż`, which is the whole of keeping the d; and `before voiced word` on an arm asks the same of a voiceless one, `k before voiced word says g`. Both compile to one rule, `letters_plpl_voiced_word_follows`, asked of the range's right end. It had to take a fence down to look. A letter rule fences its scan at the edge of the morph, which is also the word's, so the first version could never reach the next word and changed nothing; the rule lifts the fence, looks for a space and then one of the letters, and puts the fence back as the letter rule set it. And the space is asked for by its field, because a string test steps past what it has matched and that step is the one the fence refuses, so a string of one space failed where the space was plainly there.
