# Sample rates above eleven thousand

Eloquence ran at 8 kHz or 11.025 kHz and nothing else. A caller can now ask for 16, 22.05, 32, 44.1 or 48 kHz as well, and for any rate up to 48,000 given in hertz. 11.025 stays the default.

There are two ways to get there and the engine does both, but they do not sound alike and only one is the default.

**Raising the rate from 11.025, which is what the settings do.** Every rate above 11.025 kHz is produced by running the engine at 11.025 and raising the rate afterwards, so the voice is the same one at every setting. The engine is never asked for a rate it has to make up sibilants for.

**How the rate is raised is a matter for a listener, so there are five ways and `EVV_UPSAMPLE` picks.** The default is `sinc`: a windowed sinc across 192 samples, cut off at 0.98 of the input's own Nyquist under a Kaiser window, which is what a resampler actually is. `EVV_SINC_CUTOFF` and `EVV_SINC_TAPS` move those two numbers, and `src/eci/sound/eci_pcm.h` argues for the particular values.

Those numbers were got wrong first and the wrongness was audible. 0.91 over 48 samples is what soxr uses at its medium setting, and at 11,025 that puts the cutoff at 5,016 hertz -- where Eloquence still has speech. Measured, the band from 5,016 to 5,512 carries 27 dB less than the band below it, which is quiet but is not nothing, and cutting there threw away 12.4 dB of it. The voice came out duller than it should be, which is what got reported. At 0.98 over 192 that band comes through 1.1 dB under where the engine put it, and the images are still 83 dB down.

The cheaper ways came first and were not enough. `hold` repeats each sample, which keeps the mirror of the speech a resampler exists to remove -- the sound old hardware made, and what some ears want. `cubic` is a curve through four samples, the interpolation libsoxr documents its quick mode as. Measured on one sentence at 22,050, the band above the old ceiling comes to -18.0 dB with `hold`, -27.1 with `cubic` and -69.7 with the sinc; above 11 kHz at 44,100 it is -21.1, -56.2 and -83.8. So the curve makes the mirror quieter and the sinc removes it.

The passband is the other half of it, and the sinc wins there too rather than trading against it. Cubic droops: at 16,000 its 4 to 5 kHz band sits 2.8 dB below where 11,025 puts it, and the band above that 5.1 dB below. The sinc's sit 0.0 and 1.1 dB below. A curve is a poor lowpass in both directions at once; a designed filter is flat until it stops.

**Which is why the two kinds of brightness are worth telling apart.** Above the source's own Nyquist there is nothing real to have, so anything a listener hears up there is image. Below it, everything is real. Cubic is brighter than the sinc was because it leaves the images in -- 13.7 dB more energy above 5,016 hertz at 16,000, all of it mirror -- and duller than the sinc is because it droops the speech. The two move in opposite directions and a single word like brighter does not separate them. `hold` is the brightest of all in the image sense, with its mirror only 19 dB down at 16,000 against cubic's 27 and the sinc's 86.

Written here rather than linked from libsoxr because the engine has no dependency but the C library and gains none here: this ships inside a DLL a screen reader loads and inside builds for platforms nobody has put soxr on. Same arithmetic, not the same code.

**One consequence worth naming: the ratio stops mattering.** Under `hold` and `cubic`, 44,100 sounds better than 48,000 because 44,100 is exactly four times 11,025 and 48,000 is not, so 48,000 gets uneven repeats and a timing wobble. Under the sinc both measure the same to a tenth of a decibel in every band, because a filter evaluated at an arbitrary position does not care whether that position is on a grid.

**Under the ways that interpolate nothing, the engine's own samples are still there to the byte.** Under `hold` every sample is one the engine made; under `cubic` every output sample that lands on an input one -- every second at 22,050, every fourth at 44,100 -- is that sample exactly, since a curve passes through the points it is drawn through. The sinc is not held to that and should not be: it cuts the band off below the input's Nyquist, so it changes the signal on the grid as well as between, and a filter that passed every input sample through untouched would not be filtering. `make rates` runs four times over so the sinc, `cubic`, `hold` and `none` are each held to what they can claim.

**Always from 11.025, and that was measured rather than assumed.** The engine's other rate is the narrower one: at 8 kHz the cosine table runs past its own Nyquist so formants above 4,000 fold back into the speech, and it is the telephone voice rather than a quieter version of the other. 48 kHz held from it was built and tried, six exact copies of every sample, and it is that voice at 48 kHz rather than a better one -- the band from 3 to 5.5 kHz comes out 6 dB louder than 11.025 puts it and the envelope is nearly 14 dB away. Raised from 11.025 instead, 48,000 lands within 0.2 dB of 44,100 in every band. So 16,000 is taken from 11.025 too, rather than from the 8,000 IBM planned it as.

That mirror is exactly what a bandlimited resampler exists to remove, and keeping it is the point. It is the sound old hardware made, and it is what a listener means by Eloquence at 22 kHz.

This was IBM's own plan. `stw_createAudioConverter` has always chosen a native rate and stood an `AudioConverter` between it and the caller, and that converter was the one object of IBM's sound layer this port never transcribed -- so the doubling never happened and a caller asking for 22,050 got the 11,025 stream under a 22,050 label, the same speech at half the duration. `src/eci/sound/eci_pcm.c` is that object now, written as ours the way the rest of that file is, twenty-seven years later.

**Synthesising outright, which is now the comparison rather than a route anyone takes by accident.** The synthesiser was written for a rate it is given rather than for the two it knows, and it even has a computed path for a rate it does not recognise, dead in IBM's build because the two resonator tables that path needs are missing. IBM's four tables turn out to be two formulae exactly -- the decay of a pole at a bandwidth, and that pole's angle -- so another rate's are built rather than stored. `src/klatt/klatt_rates.c` is those formulae and `make rates` holds them against IBM's own arrays entry by entry: every excitation entry matches at both rates and every cosine entry at 11.025, with two differing at 8 kHz where IBM stored the saturated value a third of a count early. IBM's four are never rebuilt, so the rates that must stay byte for byte identical do not depend on our rounding.

It sounds worse, and that is why it is not the default. The frication and aspiration come from a generator that produces one value per output sample, so their bandwidth is whatever the rate is: at 44.1 kHz there is as much noise energy above 11 kHz as between 5.5 and 11, and the sibilants go thin and hissy. The same sentence at 22,050 synthesised outright has its 1,500 to 3,000 band 4 dB away from where 11,025 puts it and the new band filled with noise at 35 dB down, against the mirror's 19.

`EVV_UPSAMPLE=none` synthesises everything at its own rate, which is how the two halves are compared without a rebuild.

**Both ways of raising the rate look only backwards**, at samples already handed over, so a run joins the one before it with no seam and nothing is held back at the end. The cost is a constant delay of one input sample for linear and two for cubic, under two tenths of a millisecond. That property is the one no measurement of the audio would catch: the engine hands its samples over a few dozen at a time, so an utterance is hundreds of separate calls, and a way that did not carry its tail would put a seam at every one of them -- a buzz under the speech that would barely move a spectrum. `make rates` drives the arithmetic directly and requires a run split into uneven pieces to come out identical to the same run handed over whole.

**The gap between the engine's two rates is refused.** A rate between 8,000 and 11,025 has nothing to be taken from and cannot be synthesised safely either: the language compensates for that past-Nyquist table only at 8,000 and takes the other branch for everything else, so a rate just above 8,000 is made without the compensation it needs and the output wraps. Measured at 8,500 and 9,000, on the edge at 9,500 and 10,000, clean from about 10,500. All of the gap is refused rather than the part that misbehaves.

**The rate does not go through the language.** The annotation the rules parse takes nought or one, the rules branch on which -- `us_filtr` tests for 8,000, where the compensation for a table running past Nyquist lives -- and the cell a rule carries a rate in is sixteen bits, so 44,100 would not fit whatever the rules were taught. The engine is still told 8,000 or 11,025 and `synthesize` puts the real rate in front of the synthesiser only where the two differ. No language module changes.

**Where synthesising outright stops, and this is the one unfinished thing here.** Somewhere between 44,508 and 44,509 hertz the synthesiser's accumulator runs away on ordinary speech. One hertz moves the peak sample from about 2,800 to several million, which the caller sees as broadband noise once it has been cut to sixteen bits -- and every other measure is unchanged, so nothing but the amplitude says it happened. The boundary is the same for six of the seven varied sentences in `test/cases`, for three thousand lines of the crash corpus and for pitches from forty to a hundred hertz, so it is a constant of the rate rather than an interaction with any text. A short utterance does not reach it because it does not reach the vowel that provokes it.

What it is not, each ruled out by measurement rather than by argument. It is not the resonators: `pole_filter`'s stability condition is exactly that the gain term the coefficients are built from is positive, and the worst case of that over every frequency and bandwidth the tables can be asked for is positive at every rate up to 48 kHz. It is not the three-sample coefficient ramp, whose own gain term was measured either side of the boundary and does not cross zero. It is not the pitch period, the frame length, the voiced or open or closed lengths or the pulse amplitude, all of which are identical at 44,508 and 44,509. It is not the running sample count. Not run down further than that.

So the ceiling on synthesising outright is 44,100. It does not touch the ordinary rates at all, since the engine never leaves 11.025 for those -- which is why 48,000 is offered and yet asking for it with `EVV_UPSAMPLE=none` is refused. What synthesising outright sounds like, and why it is an experiment knob rather than a setting, is under "Measured against Apple's Eloquence" below. Widening the resonator past sixteen-bit coefficients would fix the gain error that grows with the rate -- 652 counts at 11.025 kHz, 165 at 22.05, 78 at 32 and 41 at 44.1 for a 500 hertz formant -- and might well fix the runaway with it; it is a real change to `pole_filter` and has not been made.

**Three divergences from IBM, and the first two are fixes.** IBM numbered 16 kHz rate three and then set the range of the sample rate parameter to two, so its own fourth rate could not be asked for; the range now runs to the highest rate the tables can be built for. And 22.05 and 16 kHz are doubled now rather than mislabelled. The third is the widening itself: a parameter value of four or five, or a rate in hertz, is not something IBM's engine would take.

**The wideband voice, which is the one place the engine makes sound above 5.5 kHz, and why it is built the way it is.** `eciWideband` turns it on, off by default. Synthesising higher outright was measured first and is not Eloquence higher: at 16,000 the voice comes out 3 dB down from 1.5 to 3 kHz and 5 dB down from 3 to 5.5, because a two-pole digital resonator near the top of its band passes more than the same resonator does once the band is widened under it, and five in a row add that up. Doing the resonators' arithmetic in doubles changes none of it -- that was built and measured, and is in `docs/notes/apple-eloquence.md` -- so it is the model and not the precision. Issue 40 asked for exactly that synthesis with the noise unbounded, and it would also have moved the 11,025 voice, since one of its three changes rewrote what IBM's resonator does on a zero-length call.

So the wideband voice is two synthesisers. The 11,025 one runs exactly as it always has and is raised to 22,050 by the same sinc every other rate is raised by. A companion runs the same frames at 22,050, handed each frame just before its partner, and only what it makes above the join is kept. The split is one filter used twice: the partner raised through the sinc is that filter's low side, and the companion less the same filter, drawn from the resampler's own kernel, is its high side, so the two meet without a gap or a doubling whatever either puts near the join. Below 4.5 kHz the wideband stream at 22,050 is the default one to 85 dB; at 44,100 to 83 and at 16,000 to 52, where two different converters stand between and the wideband stream arrives half a sample later. Every rate above 11,025 is taken from the 22,050 stream -- raised for 32,000 and up, lowered for 16,000, which the converter learned to do for this.

**Twice the rate, so that the two land on the same moments.** At 16,000 a five-millisecond frame rounds to a different length than at 11,025, and an unpaced companion would drift a fifth of a percent behind -- about an eighth of a second a minute, the top of every sibilant after the sibilant. At exactly twice the rate the companion takes every frame length, pitch period and flutter phase as its partner's doubled, which is what `pace_rate` in the synthesiser's state says, and its samples are taken a frame at a time so nothing can build up.

**What is up there is frication.** IBM's frames say nothing above five thousand hertz: the sixth to eighth formants sit at fixed values on every frame and their amplitudes are always nought, and the fifth never goes above 4,400. So the companion's top is the skirts of the parallel resonators an s, an sh and a z are made with, and the bypass an f and a th are. The voiced harmonics are fifty decibels down by then, and so is the aspiration under the vowels, which goes in under the formant filters as the voicing does; running the three formants IBM's synthesiser leaves out raised neither -- the band moved by a fraction of a decibel, so that was taken back out.

**How loud, and where it stops, settled by ear on 6 October 2026.** Four sentences -- one of sibilants, one of plosives, one of vowels and one Polish with s, sz, ś and cz -- at normal speed and at 400 words a minute. The top at 8, 14 and 20 decibels over what the companion makes: 14, which is about where the hiss carries on from the level just under the join. The noise stopping at 11, 9.5 and 8 kHz: 8. Two more were tried and lost. Moving the breath under the vowels did nothing worth a listen, since aspiration goes in under the formant filters as the voicing does and is as far down up there -- six decibels either way moved the output by sixty-two below the speech. And a peak for the s: IBM makes an s with the fifth formant's frication resonator at about 4,300 hertz and an sh with the third and fourth, so what lies above the join on an s is that resonator's skirt falling away; driving the unused resonator at 6,300 from the same amplitude, at three strengths up to a peak above the s's own at 4 kHz where a real s has it, was preferred to by none. `EVV_WIDE_TOP` and `EVV_WIDE_EDGE` move the two that stayed.

**What holds it.** `make wide` checks the lower band against the default at the three rates, that there is a top at all, that the top keeps pace with the band under it at the start and the end of a forty-five second text, and the setting itself. Each check was shown to fail on purpose: the setting not reaching the synthesiser, the whole companion added rather than its top, the companion one sample late, and frames handed over ten milliseconds late. Turning the pacing off is not caught and cannot be at 22,050, where the common frame rounds to exactly twice its partner's anyway; what pacing guards there is the flutter phase once a long-lived instance's sample count wraps, and frame lengths whose fraction rounds the other way. The matrix's `wide` category records it at 16,000 in every language.

**In whole numbers, as IBM's synthesiser is.** The converter, the join and the companion's noise filter were first written in doubles, and the gate's thirty-two bit Linux build moved the wide cases, for two reasons. The Kaiser window is not nought at its ends, so the filter steps there, and lowering 22,050 to 16,000 put a tap exactly on the step in two output samples of every 320; whether it counted came down to the last bit of a rounded product, which x87 kept and every other processor dropped. And the engine's near-silence is a small repeating pattern, -2, -1, 0, -1, -2, -3, whose symmetry puts some samples exactly halfway between two counts once the rate is raised, so they rounded whichever way the last bit fell. Compiling the thirty-two bit builds for SSE2 would have mended the gate and nothing else: a DOS machine has x87 or no floating point at all, most of Rockbox's players have none, and PowerPC and ARM compilers fuse a multiply and an add into one rounding unless told not to. So the filter is drawn in whole numbers -- sine, square root and Bessel function alike, within 26 parts in 2^30 of the same filter drawn in doubles -- and its weights are worked out once for every place an output sample can fall between two input ones, each place's coming to exactly one; the join adds in sixty-four bit integers; and the noise filter is a fixed-point biquad pair, designed in whole numbers when the rate is set, with its state in the synthesiser's own block rather than in a table of eight shared by every instance. Every build gives the same bytes without asking anything of its compiler: twenty renders at five rates, with the wideband voice and without, came out identical from the sixty-four bit build, the thirty-two bit Linux build on plain x87 and the thirty-two bit Windows build. Against the doubles, the default voice moved in a few dozen samples a minute by one count, 116 dB under the voice, and the wideband voice in about one sample in two hundred by up to twelve, 81 dB under: the companion's noise differs by a count now and then and its resonators carry that on. What is left in floating point is the resonator tables, worked out once at a rate IBM did not ship, the little the Frenches' rules do, and the experiment knobs that take a number in decibels or a fraction.

**What it costs.** Over seventy-two seconds of English, the default voice took 0.9 seconds of one core at 22,050, 1.1 at 16,000 and 2.5 at 44,100, where in doubles with the weights worked out tap by tap it took 5.1, 3.6 and 8.8. The wideband voice took 2.4, 3.5 and 4.4: the companion is a second synthesiser at twice the rate, and the join a second filter, so it costs two to three times the default at the same rate and a sixteenth of a core at most. Worked out ahead of time the weights take memory -- a megabyte raising 11,025 to 32,000, which shares the least with it of any numbered rate, a third of one lowering 22,050 to 16,000, and a kilobyte or two for the whole ratios.

Two bugs found on the way. `ev_concatVoice` indexes the concatenative voice table by rate with three rate slots to a dialect, and IBM's own rate three already ran past the dialect into the next one; it answers no entry for a rate the table has no slot for. And `stb_synthCallback` decides whether to convert by reading a byte at offset 0x2ac of the concatenation manager, which is allocated with plain `malloc` and was never written -- so the question was decided on whatever was in that memory. It mattered not at all while there was no converter to skip and would have decided it at random the moment there was one. `cm_ctor` clears the whole block now and names that byte.

The NVDA add-on offers the rates as a setting of its own, called sample rate to keep it apart from NVDA's own rate, which is the speed. The engine and the player are moved together or not at all, and anything that will not build puts both back: an engine at one rate feeding a player made for another is speech at the wrong speed and neither half reports it. `nvda/test/engine.py` covers that, including a player that refuses to be built.
